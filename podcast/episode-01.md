# Episode 1: Teaching a walker to take real steps

Hi, and welcome to the first episode of the Bipedal CMA-ES podcast. This is where I tell you, in plain words, what we're trying to do, what has worked, what hasn't, and what comes next.

Let's start with the project. We have a little two-legged robot in a physics simulation. It has a body, two legs, a hip and a knee on each leg, and a bumpy track that's different every time. Its brain is a small neural network with nine hundred and thirty two numbers in it. We don't train those numbers with gradients. We evolve them, with an algorithm called CMA-ES. It tries a cloud of slightly different walkers, keeps the ones that do best, and slowly reshapes the cloud toward better walkers.

So, what worked? The first big result came on Tuesday. After three false starts, the walker learned to walk the whole course. On a hundred tracks it had never seen, it scored two hundred and sixty four points on average. That isn't the three hundred that counts as solved, but it's well ahead of my earlier walker, which was evolved with a method called NEAT. On the same hundred tracks, the new walker scored about ninety points more, and it was better on ninety three of them.

The false starts are worth a moment, because they taught us the most important lesson of the whole project. Evolution climbs whatever you reward, and it takes the nearest way up. In the first attempt, the walker learned to dive forward and fall. In the second, it learned to lunge and fall. In the third, it fell on purpose, because every second alive cost it energy. Each time, the reward was quietly saying that falling was the cheapest answer. When we made a fall cost twenty points in training, standing became the safe choice, and from standing, every step forward paid off. That's when it learned to walk.

Now for the part that didn't work. When you watch the walker, something looks wrong. It doesn't really walk. It skips. One leg always stays in front, reaching forward, and the other leg pushes from behind. The feet never pass each other. And it's not just this walker. My old NEAT walker does the same thing. Even a random network that only stands there keeps the same foot in front.

Why? Because the reward doesn't care. It pays for distance, it charges for effort and for falling, and that's all. A skip that covers the ground cheaply is just as good as a real walk. So evolution found the nearest gait that works and stopped there.

You asked for a walker that puts one foot in front of the other, and fast. So since Wednesday morning, that's the goal. Here's what I've tried, and how each attempt failed.

First, I changed the network. One shared controller drives both legs, with a clock that runs half a cycle apart for each leg. The idea was that neither leg could become a permanent prop. It didn't work. Each leg can see where the other leg is, so the same controller learned two roles: if I'm in front, reach, and if I'm behind, push. It still skipped.

Next, I changed the reward, with a penalty for one foot leading all the time. Starting from the skipping walker, it just kept skipping and paid the penalty. Starting from scratch, something interesting happened: it really did alternate its feet, left, right, left. But it fell every time, after about fourteen units. I'd also made a mistake: my penalty punished standing still with the feet apart, so stumbling forward and falling looked better than standing. Fixing that, by charging the penalty per distance walked, gave a walker that just stood still. And restarting from the alternating stumbler kept it alternating, but it still fell every time.

That's when you suggested a curriculum, and I think it's the right idea. A child doesn't learn balance, stepping and speed all at once. So the curriculum has four stages, each with its own reward. First, balance. Then, stepping. Then, walking. Then, walking fast. Each stage starts from the champion of the stage before.

The first curriculum attempt passed the balance stage quickly. But the champion stood with its feet almost three units apart, one foot always in front: exactly the skipper's stance. And in the stepping stage, it found a new shortcut. My reward paid for touchdowns that alternate between the feet. So it learned to tap its feet, left, right, left, about seventy times per episode, without ever moving them past each other. Technically alternating, but not a single real step.

So here's what's running now, in the second attempt. Balance has to be done with the feet close together, so that either leg can take the first step. And in the stepping stage, a touchdown only counts as a step when the foot coming down is the front foot, and the legs take turns. That's what a real step looks like: you swing your back foot forward and put it down in front. Tapping in a split stance now earns almost nothing. The old tapper drops from about a hundred and five points to forty seven, while a walker that really steps, without falling, could earn about three hundred.

What comes next? If the stepping stage passes, the walker moves on to walking, where distance pays, and a lopsided gait is taxed in proportion to how far it goes. Then the last stage, walking fast, uses the real game reward plus the same gait tax, so it keeps its form while it speeds up. The goal check is strict. On twenty tracks it has never seen, the feet have to take turns leading, with at least twenty changes per run. It has to get past eighty units of the roughly eighty nine unit course, fall no more than twice, and score at least two hundred and fifty points.

I'll check in every two hours until Friday at five in the afternoon. If a stage gets stuck or finds another shortcut, I'll look at the data, change the reward, and start a new attempt. You can follow all of it on the curriculum page: each stage's reward, what it's supposed to learn, what it actually did, replays of every champion, and a running journal.

I'll be honest about the odds. Real walking is a narrow target, and evolution is very good at finding loopholes. But every failure so far has told us something specific, and the curriculum is the first approach that breaks the problem into pieces evolution can actually climb. That's it for episode one. Thanks for listening.
