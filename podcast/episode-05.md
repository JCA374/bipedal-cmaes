# Episode 5: A correction, and the price of speed

This is episode five, recorded just after midnight on Thursday, and it starts with a correction.

In the last episode I told you our best walker scored about two hundred and eighty eight, with no falls at all. That came from twenty test tracks. Tonight I checked the two best walkers on a hundred tracks instead, both on the very same tracks. And the picture changed. The wider network with cheaper steps scored about two hundred and sixty three on average, and it fell on fourteen of the hundred tracks. The newest run, the one that picks its champions more carefully, scored about two hundred and sixty seven, and fell on twelve. The difference between those two is just noise.

Here's what that means. The walkers really did get faster. They now finish in about eight hundred and eighty steps, and their effort is down to about seventy three. But yesterday afternoon's walker, which was slower, scored about two hundred and sixty four on a hundred tracks with a spread of only one point, and it never fell. So the extra speed was bought with safety. On average, the score hasn't really gone up. It has just become riskier.

Why didn't I see this earlier? It's the winner's curse again, the same lesson as in lesson four, and this time it caught my own goal check. When falls are rare, twenty tracks can easily contain none of the bad ones. A walker that falls on one track in eight will often look perfect on twenty.

So I've changed three things. First, the goal check now uses fifty tracks instead of twenty, and the walker may fall on at most one track in ten. That's the same standard as before, just measured properly. On that check, the wider network with cheaper steps still meets the goal: about two hundred and seventy two points, four falls in fifty, and more than a hundred front-foot changes per run. The careful-selection run doesn't: it fell nine times.

Second, I stopped the wider network's run and started a new one from its champion where a fall costs twice as much during training. The idea is to buy the safety back, without giving up all the speed.

Third, I'm checking big claims on a hundred tracks before I tell you about them.

What's next: the safety run trains through the night, and the careful-selection run keeps going alongside it. If the safety run keeps the speed and cuts the falls, we'll have a walker that's both fast and safe, and a score that really is higher. If it simply slows down, that tells us the speed and the safety trade off against each other in this gait, and I'll try something different, like a small memory, or a deeper network. Thanks for listening, and sorry for the over-excited headline last time.
