Two ways to walk

The champion scores well, but it never puts one foot in front of the other: one leg always leads. This lesson looks at why evolution settled on that gait, and tests a change to the network's structure that should make real, alternating steps the natural thing to learn.

After this lesson you can
  • measure a gait: touchdown order, which foot leads, and how often the lead changes;
  • explain why a local search keeps a gait that works, even when a better one exists;
  • describe how a shared controller with a phase-shifted clock builds alternation into the network.

2Why evolution kept it

Nothing in the reward asks for crossing steps. It pays for distance and charges for effort and falls. The skipping gait, with leg 2 always reaching forward and leg 1 pushing from behind, earns about as much as a "real" walk would.

And CMA-ES searches locally: it samples a cloud around its current best guess. To switch gaits, leg 2 would have to start swinging back past leg 1, and the walkers in between are clumsy and score worse. That's a valley in the fitness landscape. Late in training the cloud is small (σ ≈ 0.05), so it never samples far enough to see the other side. Evolution keeps the nearest gait that works, not the one we'd pick.

3The fix: one controller, two legs, a clock

Instead of adding a reward and hoping, change what the network can express:

  • One controller for both legs. A small network sees the body, its own leg, the other leg and the lidar, and outputs a hip and a knee command. Leg 1 uses it with its own view; leg 2 uses the very same weights with the two legs swapped. Neither leg can become a specialist prop, because there's only one set of weights.
  • A clock. The controller also gets sin φ and cos φ of a phase that cycles every T steps. Leg 2's clock runs half a cycle (π) behind. T is learned along with the weights.
  • A small training bonus (0.5 points) for each step where the front foot changes and the body has moved forward at least 0.4 units since the last one. Shuffling in place earns nothing, and reported scores stay raw.

The size barely changes: 26 inputs → 32 hidden → 2 outputs, plus the clock period, is 931 numbers instead of 932.

Why the half-cycle shift matters

An illustration (not the physics simulation): two legs driven by the same rule, hip angle = A · sin(φ + shift). With shift 0 the legs move together, which is a hop. With shift π they take turns, and the feet pass each other on every step. The real controller is a whole network rather than a sine wave, but the clock's half-cycle offset gives it the same push.

Why not just a symmetric controller?Without the clock, two legs that start in the same position get exactly the same inputs, so a shared controller gives them the same commands forever: a perfect hop. The simulation is deterministic, so nothing breaks the tie. The phase shift is what makes the legs different.

Verdict

Check yourself

1. A walker alternates touchdowns 1-2-1-2, but foot 2 is in front 100% of the time. What is it doing?

Alternating touchdowns isn't enough. In a walk, the leading foot changes on every step.

2. Why didn't more training turn the skip into a walk?

A fitness valley separates the two gaits; a local search with a small σ doesn't cross it.

3. What does the half-cycle clock shift do in the shared controller?

Same weights + opposite phase = the same movement, taking turns. Without the shift, identical legs would move identically.
