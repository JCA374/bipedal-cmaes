Lesson 1 of 8

The task

Before we can talk about how a network learns to walk, we need to be exact about what it can see, what it can do, and what counts as doing well.

After this lesson you can
  • name the 24 numbers the walker senses and the 4 numbers it outputs;
  • work out where the score of an episode comes from, and why 300 means "solved";
  • explain why the 1600-step time limit matters as much as falling.

1Meet the walker

BipedalWalker-v3 is a small 2-D physics simulation (Box2D). A box-shaped body, the hull, sits on two legs. Each leg has two joints, a hip and a knee, and each joint has a motor. The ground is a bumpy grass track, generated from a random seed, so it is different every episode. The walker starts on the left and has to get to the finish line on the right, about 84 units away.

The simulation runs at 50 steps per second. An episode ends when the walker falls (the hull touches the ground), reaches the finish, or uses up 1600 steps (32 seconds of simulated time).

Watch it, one step at a time

The orange lines are the lidar rays. Dots on the feet light up when a foot touches the ground. The bars on the right are the exact numbers the network receives and sends at this step.

The "trained walker" is the champion from my previous project (NEAT, about 173 on average). Once the CMA-ES walker is trained it replaces it here.

2What it senses: 24 numbers

At every step the walker gets an observation: a list of 24 numbers. It never sees a picture. This is everything it knows:

#WhatTells it
0–3hull angle, hull angular velocity, velocity x, velocity yam I tilting, spinning, moving forward, bouncing?
4–8leg 1: hip angle, hip speed, knee angle, knee speed, foot contactwhere leg 1 is and whether its foot is on the ground
9–13leg 2: the same fivethe same for leg 2
14–2310 lidar distanceshow far away the ground is along 10 rays, from straight down to almost straight ahead

The raw numbers have different ranges, so each one is divided by a fixed constant and clipped to [−1, 1]. The scaling never changes during training, so a trained walker is fully described by its weights alone.

ImportantNotice what is missing: the walker gets no x position, no clock and no memory. A plain feed-forward network has to decide each step from this snapshot alone. If it needs a rhythm, the rhythm has to come out of the legs' own angles and speeds.

3What it does: 4 numbers

The action is four numbers in [−1, 1], one per joint: hip 1, knee 1, hip 2, knee 2. The sign says which way to turn the joint; the size says how hard to push (up to a maximum torque of 80). That's all the control there is: four motor commands, 50 times per second.

4How it's scored

The reward at each step has three parts:

reward = 130 · Δx / 30  −  5 · Δ|hull angle|  −  0.028 · (|a₁|+|a₂|+|a₃|+|a₄|)
  • Progress: moving the hull forward earns reward. Walking the whole course is worth about 360.
  • Keep the head level: tilting the hull costs a little.
  • Effort: every unit of motor command costs 0.028 per step. Pushing all four joints at full power for 1600 steps would cost 179; a good walker spends about 30–60.
  • Falling: if the hull touches the ground, that step's reward becomes −100 and the episode ends.

So a walker that crosses the finish without falling scores about 360 − effort ≈ 300–330. The environment counts as solved when the average over 100 random tracks is ≥ 300. A walker that falls halfway scores about 180 − 100 − effort ≈ 50: the fall wipes out more than half of what it earned.

The hidden constraint: timeAbout 84 units in 1600 steps means you have to average 2.6 units per second. My NEAT walker fell on only 9% of tracks but still scored only about 175, because it ran out of time around x ≈ 74. To reach 300 you need to be both safe and fast, and those two pull against each other: fast walkers tend to pitch forward and fall.

5The job, stated precisely

We want a function, called a policy, that maps 24 numbers to 4 numbers:

π(observation) → action     ℝ²⁴ → [−1, 1]⁴

and makes the total reward over an episode as large as possible, on tracks it has never seen. Lesson 2 shows what this function looks like (a small neural network), and lesson 3 shows how to find good settings for it without ever computing a gradient.

Check yourself

1. The walker stands perfectly still for all 1600 steps without falling. Roughly what does it score?

Reward comes from progress. With no progress it only pays the small effort cost, so it ends just below zero. Not falling isn't rewarded by itself.

2. Which input could tell the walker that a bump is coming up ahead?

The lidar rays fan out forward and measure the distance to the ground, so a bump ahead shortens the forward rays before the walker gets there.

3. A walker never falls but scores only 175 on average. What's the most likely reason?

Progress is worth about 360 for the full course. Stopping at x ≈ 74 out of 88 when the 1600 steps run out leaves a lot of that reward behind. Effort costs only 30–60.
