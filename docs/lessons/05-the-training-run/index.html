Lesson 6 of 9

The training run

CMA-ES climbs whatever you tell it to climb. The first three attempts climbed the wrong hill: the walker learned to dive, to lunge, and to fall on purpose. This lesson is about why, and what it took to get a walker.

After this lesson you can
  • explain the difference between the training fitness and the reported score;
  • predict which behaviour a fitness function rewards, and spot a local optimum it creates;
  • read a training curve: fitness, step size σ and falls.

1What CMA-ES actually climbs

The reported score is fixed: the environment's raw reward over a full 1600-step episode (lesson 1). The training fitness is ours to design, and it differs in two ways:

  • Early stop: if the body moves less than 1 unit in 150 steps, the episode is cut short. A walker that's standing still can't teach us anything new, and cutting saves a lot of simulation time.
  • Shaping: the price of a fall (normally −100) and of a stall can be changed, for training only.

These look like small bookkeeping choices. They decide everything.

2Three false starts

Every run starts the same way: random networks that fall over within about 60 steps. From there, CMA-ES follows whichever direction ranks better. Here is what each attempt converged to.

runfall coststall costwhat it learned
v0100 (raw)100dive: throw the body forward and fall
v10100lunge: one big step, then fall
v200fall early on purpose
v3200stand, then step, then walk
  • v0: the stall cost made standing still (≈ −108) worse than falling (≈ −98). Falling forward was the best option, and every dive scored about the same, so there was no slope to follow.
  • v1: with a free fall, "lunge, catch yourself, stand" should win. But the stall detector cut it short and charged 100.
  • v2: with both free, it still fell. The reason is the effort cost: a random network pushes all four motors hard, about 0.11 per step. Falling at step 50 ends that bill; standing for 150+ steps until the stall detector fires costs about 8. Falling was simply cheaper.
  • v3: a fall cost of 20 is more than the effort a fall saves, but far less than the −100 that made my NEAT walker afraid to take a step. Standing became the safe choice, and from standing, every step forward added reward.

Design the fitness yourself

Each row is a real policy from these runs, scored with the training rollout (early stop on) on the same 20 training tracks. Move the sliders: the table recomputes each behaviour's training fitness as raw + (100 − fall cost) · fell − stall cost · stalled and ranks them. CMA-ES drifts toward whatever is on top among the behaviours it can reach from where it is.

The lessonThe walker always reaches the finish line in the long run by not falling, but evolution only sees the next small step. A good fitness function makes every small improvement on the way to walking score a little better than the one before: random flailing → standing → a step → several steps → walking. If one link in that chain scores worse than the one before it, the search stops there.

3The run that worked

With fall cost 20, the first ~50 generations look like nothing is happening: candidates learn to stand and shuffle. Then, within about ten generations, the best training fitness jumps from about 0 to over 120, and the average distance from 6 to 16 units. The first real steps. The step size σ stays large through all of this: CMA-ES is still exploring.

Check yourself

1. Falls cost 100 and stalls cost nothing. A random walker can either fall right away or stand still. Which does training prefer?

That's NEAT's trap: standing is far better than any risky step, so the search can get stuck standing still.

2. Why did run v2 (no fall cost, no stall cost) still learn to fall?

A random network uses a lot of torque. Every extra step alive costs effort, so the cheapest episode was a short one.
