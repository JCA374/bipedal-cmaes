Lesson 5 of 9

The reward function

The walker never sees our goal ("walk well"). It sees one number per step, and learns whatever makes the total of those numbers large. This lesson reads that number line by line: what it pays for, what it doesn't, and what that does to the gait.

After this lesson you can
  • write down the exact reward and split an episode's score into its parts;
  • say what the reward does not pay for, and predict the side effects;
  • explain why a reward is only a proxy for what we want, and how the walkers here "gamed" it.

1The actual code

This is the reward from the environment's source (gymnasium 0.29, bipedal_walker.py), lightly trimmed. It runs once per simulation step, 50 times per second:

shaping  = 130 * hull.x / SCALE          # SCALE = 30: distance, in points
shaping -= 5.0 * abs(hull_angle)          # keep the body level
reward   = shaping - prev_shaping         # only the CHANGE counts each step
prev_shaping = shaping

for a in action:                          # four motor commands
    reward -= 0.00035 * 80 * min(abs(a), 1)   # = 0.028 per unit of effort

if hull touches the ground or x < 0:
    reward = -100                         # replaces this step's reward
    terminated = True
if hull.x > finish:                        # x ≈ 88.7
    terminated = True                     # no bonus for finishing

Four parts, then:

partper stepover a whole episode
progress+4.33 per unit moved forward≈ 364 from start (x = 4.7) to finish (x = 88.7)
effort−0.028 × (|a₁|+|a₂|+|a₃|+|a₄|)0 to −179 (all motors flat out for 1600 steps)
tilt−5 × change in |hull angle|tiny: only the final tilt counts, since the changes add up
fall−100, episode over−100 plus the progress you never made

2Where each walker's score comes from

Split recorded episodes into those parts. Green is what the walker earned by moving, orange what it spent on effort, red the fall. The numbers add up to the real score, within a point.

3What it does not pay for

not in the rewardso…
footsteps, or how many steps you takea shuffle and a stride over the same distance earn the same
style: feet passing, symmetry, a human-like gaita skip that covers ground cheaply is as good as a walk (lesson 9)
staying aliveno points for standing; not falling only pays through the progress you keep making
finishingno bonus at the line; finishing early only saves effort you'd otherwise spend
time, directlybut every step costs effort, so slow walking is expensive: the reward quietly favours speed
The reward is a proxyWe want "walk well". The reward says "move right, spend little, don't touch the ground". An optimizer doesn't know the difference, and it will find any loophole between the two. In this project it found several: diving forward, lunging, falling early to stop paying for effort, standing still (NEAT), and skipping with one leg as a prop. Each was a perfectly good answer to the question the reward actually asked.

5Reward vs training fitness

Everything on this site is reported with the reward above, unchanged. But during training we're allowed to optimise something slightly different, the training fitness, if that leads the search to a better walker on the real reward. Lesson 6 shows how much that choice matters: the first three attempts used fitness functions that made falling the best thing to do.

Check yourself

1. Two walkers cover the same distance; one takes 60 steps, the other 120. Who gets more reward?

There's no term for footsteps. Fewer, bigger steps only help if they cost less effort.

2. Why does a slow but safe walker score poorly even if it never falls?

Time isn't penalised directly, but effort accumulates per step and progress stops at the time limit.

3. The walker skips instead of walking. Is that a bug in the learning?

The optimizer did its job. If we want a particular gait, we have to ask for it, in the reward or in the network's structure (lesson 9).
