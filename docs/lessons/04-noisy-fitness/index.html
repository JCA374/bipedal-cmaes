Lesson 4 of 9

Noisy fitness, honest selection

A walker's score depends on the track it gets. Every time we measure fitness, we're rolling dice. This lesson is about not fooling yourself, during training and when you claim a result.

After this lesson you can
  • explain the "winner's curse": why the best-looking candidate is usually worse than it looks;
  • say what common random numbers are and why they make ranking fairer;
  • describe how this project picks its champion and checks its final score.

1The same walker, different scores

Each track is generated from a random seed. Some are gentle, some have awkward bumps exactly where the walker puts its foot down. In my NEAT project, a single walker's score varied by about ±65 from one track to the next. That's huge compared with the differences we care about: 280 vs 300 is "nearly solved" vs "solved".

So a fitness measurement is really true skill + luck. Averaging over K tracks shrinks the luck by a factor of √K: with ±65 per track, 5 tracks give about ±29 and 100 tracks about ±6.5. More tracks cost more time, though: every generation already runs 48 candidates × 5 tracks = 240 episodes.

2The winner's curse

Now pick the best-looking candidate out of 48. It's probably good, but it's also probably the one that got the luckiest tracks. Selecting on a noisy measurement favours luck, so the winner's measured score is inflated. Measure it again on fresh tracks and it drops.

Pick a champion under noise

48 candidates with true skill spread between 150 and 250 (unknown to the selector). Each is measured on K tracks with ±65 noise per track. We pick the top-scoring one and then re-measure it on 100 fresh tracks.

Things to try (numbers from 4,000 simulated runs):

  • K = 1, run 500 times: the winner looks about 80 points better than it is, and the truly best candidate is picked only about 10% of the time.
  • K = 5 (what training uses): the inflation drops to about 30, and a top-5 candidate is picked about two times in three.
  • K = 20: about 12 points of inflation, a top-5 pick 87% of the time.
  • At K = 5, untick "same tracks": the inflation rises from 30 to about 50, and top-5 picks drop from 66% to 51%. That's the next section.

3Common random numbers

Part of the luck is shared: an awkward track is awkward for everyone. In the demo, each track has a difficulty that shifts every candidate's score, plus a smaller part that depends on the candidate. If every candidate in a generation walks the same 5 tracks, the shared part shifts all scores equally and cancels out of the ranking. Only the candidate-specific part still adds noise.

This trick is called common random numbers. It costs nothing: same number of episodes, fairer comparisons. train.py does exactly this: one set of 5 seeds per generation, drawn fresh each generation so no candidate can overfit to particular tracks.

And CMA-ES only needs the rankingRecall lesson 3: CMA-ES never uses the scores themselves, only their order. With common random numbers, a hard generation (all scores low) and an easy one (all scores high) produce the same kind of update.

4How the champion is chosen

The training fitness is noisy (5 tracks) and shaped (lessons 5 and 6 explain the shaping). So the champion is never chosen from it directly. Instead:

  1. Every 10 generations, take the CMA mean and the best 3 candidates of that generation.
  2. Re-test each on the same 20 held-out tracks, never used in training, with full 1600-step episodes and the raw reward.
  3. The best of these on the held-out tracks becomes the champion if it beats the previous one.

Why include the CMA mean? It isn't a candidate anyone sampled; it's the centre of the cloud, an average of many good candidates. Averaging tends to cancel out quirks that only worked on lucky tracks, so the mean is often more robust than any single sample.

Even 20 tracks have about ±15 of noise, and picking the best of many re-tests on the same 20 tracks brings the winner's curse back. That's why the final number comes from a separate test.

5Claiming a result

PurposeTrack seedsUsed for
training0 – 999,999fitness, 5 fresh tracks per generation
hall of fame10,000,000+choosing the champion (20 tracks)
evaluation20,000,000+the final score (100 tracks)
recording30,000,000+videos and examples
comparisons40,000,000+paired comparisons between two walkers

The ranges never overlap, and a test checks that. The headline number is the mean over 100 tracks the walker has never seen, from full episodes with the unmodified reward. In my NEAT project this mattered: the champion that scored "200" during selection scored 180 on 20 new tracks and 173–179 on 100.

To compare two walkers, both run on the same 100 new tracks (common random numbers again), and the result is the average difference with a bootstrap confidence interval. A difference smaller than about ±20 is usually noise.

Check yourself

1. The top candidate of a generation scored 290 on its 5 training tracks. What's the best guess for its score on 100 new tracks?

It was selected because it scored highest, so part of that 290 is luck. On fresh tracks it regresses toward its true skill.

2. What do common random numbers remove?

The shared difficulty cancels out of the comparison. The candidate-specific part, and the winner's curse, remain.

3. Why does the champion search include the CMA mean?

The mean is the centre of the search cloud, which smooths out single lucky solutions. It isn't always the best, so it competes with the top 3.
